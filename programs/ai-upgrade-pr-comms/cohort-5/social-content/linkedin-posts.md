## Post 2: The Four Ethics Landmines (Framework Post)

**Headline**: The Four Ethics Landmines Every PR Team Needs to Navigate

I've taught hundreds of communications professionals to use AI.

Every cohort asks the same question: "How do I use this ethically?"

Here's the framework we developed after reviewing AI policies from six major PR firms and watching where teams actually stumble:

**🚨 Landmine 1: Hallucinations**

AI will state wrong things with supreme confidence. It has no ability to say "I don't know."

*The rule:* Human in the loop for anything client-facing or public. Grounded tools and RAG help, but verification always takes time. Build that into your workflows.

**🚨 Landmine 2: Copyright**

Your AI ingests data. That data may or may not be properly licensed.

*The rule:* Internal use ≠ public use. Consider the reputational risk, not just legal protection.

**🚨 Landmine 3: Bias and Sycophancy**

Here's what "AI bias" actually means: embedded racism, sexism, misogyny, homophobia, and ableism at a systematic level.

AI data skews Western and English-speaking. And it wants to please you—it will reinforce your existing assumptions, not challenge them.

*The rule:* Don't ask AI for critical feedback expecting honesty. Build prompts that counteract sycophancy. Name the biases explicitly.

**🚨 Landmine 4: Privacy**

Even if your data isn't used for training, it exists on vendor platforms. Hacks happen. Legal compulsion happens.

*The rule:* Treat AI like any cloud platform. Apply appropriate sensitivity based on data type.

**The through-line across all four:**

Every PR firm we reviewed emphasizes transparency, human oversight, and (increasingly) environmental responsibility.

## Post 4: From Outputs to Collaboration (Mindset Shift)

**Headline**: You're Using AI Wrong If You're Treating It Like Santa Claus

When AI chatbots first launched, we all did the same thing:

"Write me an email."
"Create this image."
"Draft this press release."

We treated AI like Santa Claus—a magical being who gives you presents when you ask nicely.

**That's not how the best practitioners use AI anymore.**

The biggest shift in AI over the past 18 months isn't better models (though they're better). It's the move from **outputs** to **collaboration**.

Here's the difference:

**Output mindset:** "AI, write me a crisis statement."

**Collaboration mindset:** "I need to respond to a product issue trending on social. The CEO wants something in 20 minutes. I have internal notes with unverified details and employee names. Help me think through: What can I safely include? What needs verification? What's the appropriate tone?"

One treats AI as a vending machine. The other treats AI as a thinking partner.

**Why this matters:**

AI has become that thinking partner to go to when you simply just need one and you don't have a human handy.

Like how we started Googling everything when search engines matured.

Like how we started watching YouTube tutorials for anything we needed to learn.

AI is becoming the default first stop for **working through problems**—not just generating content.

**For your workflows:**

- Stop asking AI to produce final outputs
- Start looping AI into your thinking process EARLY
- Use it to stress-test ideas, not just execute them

The best outputs come from the best collaboration.

Not from better prompts. From better partnerships.

## Post 5: Bias Isn't Innocuous (Ethics Deep-Dive)

**Headline**: When We Say "AI Bias," We Mean Racism, Sexism, and Homophobia. Let's Stop Softening the Language.

In Cohort 5 this week, I said something that made people uncomfortable:

"When we say bias, it sounds really innocuous—like everyone has preferences.

But we're talking about having to overcome embedded racism, sexism, misogyny, homophobia, ableism—all the big isms of our society are embedded into the tools at a very systematic level."

**Why I don't soften this:**

AI is trained on internet data. That data reflects our culture's worst patterns alongside its best ones.

When AI skews Western and English-speaking, that's not just "cultural context." It's structural exclusion of entire perspectives.

When AI reinforces your existing assumptions (because it wants to satisfy your prompt), it's amplifying whatever biases YOU bring to the conversation.

**What this means for comms professionals:**

1. **There is no "neutral" AI.** Every model has a mandate, a training set, and an interpretation of truth. Don't assume objectivity.

2. **Bias detection is part of your job now.** Just like fact-checking sources, you need to check AI outputs for embedded assumptions.

3. **Your prompts shape the bias.** If you ask for "professional" language, you may get code-switching that erases cultural voice. If you don't specify diverse perspectives, you won't get them.

4. **Mitigation requires active effort.** Prompts like "consider perspectives I might be missing" or "challenge my assumptions" can help—but they're not foolproof.

**The uncomfortable truth:**

AI doesn't fix our problems. It scales them.

If your organization hasn't done the DEI work, AI will amplify whatever biases already exist in your culture.

The tools are only as equitable as the humans wielding them.

**For ethics policies:**

Stop using "bias" as a catch-all. Name the specific harms you're trying to prevent. Build detection mechanisms into your review processes.

The conversation has to be uncomfortable to be effective.

## Post 6: Why Your Best People Are Skeptical About AI (Counter-Intuitive Insight)

**Headline**: If Your Senior Team Is Skeptical About AI, That's Actually a Good Sign

Here's something counterintuitive I've noticed across five cohorts:

**The more expertise someone has, the less impressed they are by AI outputs.**

Andrea, an agency owner with decades of experience, joined Cohort 5 with this observation:

"We're using AI. It's streamlining things. But we frequently get clients who say 'With AI, you should get all of this done.' Meanwhile, most editors we work with are mad at AI."

She's caught in the gap between inflated expectations and professional skepticism.

**Why this happens:**

AI produces average-quality outputs based on internet-scale data. Out of the box, it's calibrated for "passable" not "excellent."

- If you're a novice, passable looks amazing
- If you're a professional, passable looks... passable

Your senior people aren't being resistant. They're applying quality standards that took years to develop.

**The reframe:**

AI provides more value to novices than professionals—out of the box.

But professionals who learn to collaborate with AI (not just extract outputs from it) unlock a different tier of value.

They use AI to:
- Accelerate their existing workflows
- Stress-test their professional judgment
- Handle the 80% that doesn't need their full attention so they can focus on the 20% that does

**For managers:**

Don't dismiss senior skepticism. Channel it.

Send your skeptics to domain-specific AI training (not generic "AI overview" sessions). Let them apply professional standards to the tools.

The best AI educators are often the ones who started skeptical. They protect the craft while embracing the capabilities.

Sarah, a former Wall Street Journal journalist in Cohort 5, told us: "I've done shockingly little with AI. As a journalist, I just ignored it completely and thought it couldn't help."

Six weeks from now, she'll know exactly where it helps and where it doesn't.

That's the transformation we're after.
